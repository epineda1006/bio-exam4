Exam moves

How to grab points.

  • Counting questions

    Write 2n first. Then ask two things: have the chromosomes copied yet (S phase done)? Have the copies split apart yet (anaphase)? The table does the rest.

  • Multiple choice

    Cross out the two silly options first. Watch for swapped words like chromosome vs chromatid, homolog vs sister, transcription vs translation.

  • Fill-in

    Spell the key word right: kinetochore, centromere, cytokinesis, chiasmata, Okazaki, spliceosome.

  • Short answer

    Three beats: name it, say what happens, say why it matters. A small sketch with labels often earns the point by itself.

  • Probability

    AND means multiply. OR means add. Do each gene alone, then combine.

Big ideas

Read these out loud.

  • Cell Division

    G1 grow, S copy DNA, G2 get ready, then PMAT and split the cytoplasm. Two identical cells, same 2n as the parent.

    Checkpoints at G1, G2 and M. Cyclins plus Cdks push forward; Rb, p53 and p21 brake. Broken brakes or a stuck gas pedal can lead to cancer.

    DNA copying is semiconservative: helicase unzips, primase primes, polymerase builds 5' to 3', ligase glues the Okazaki pieces.

  • Meiosis

    One copy of DNA, two divisions, four haploid cells that are all different. Meiosis I splits the couples (homologs), meiosis II splits the twins (sisters).

    Variety comes from crossing over (prophase I), independent assortment (2n combos at metaphase I) and random fertilization.

  • Inheritance

    Aa x Aa gives 3:1 by look and 1:2:1 by genes. AaBb x AaBb gives 9:3:3:1. Test cross uses the recessive homozygote.

    Genes close together on one chromosome are linked. Recombination frequency of 1% = 1 map unit. Nondisjunction makes trisomy or monosomy.

  • DNA to Traits

    DNA to RNA (transcription, nucleus) to protein (translation, ribosome). Codons are 3 bases; AUG starts; UAA, UAG, UGA stop.

    Pre-mRNA gets a 5' cap, a poly-A tail and its introns spliced out before it leaves the nucleus.

  • Gene Control

    Eukaryotes control genes at five levels: epigenetic, transcription, after transcription, translation, after translation.

    Acetylation opens chromatin (on). Methylation of DNA closes it (off). microRNAs shut down mRNAs.

  • Biotech

    DNA is negative, so it runs to the positive end of a gel; small pieces go farthest. PCR doubles DNA every cycle: 2n.

    Cloning: cut with a restriction enzyme, paste into a plasmid with ligase, put into bacteria, select with antibiotics.

Sleep is part of studying. Memory locks in overnight.

Cell division.

Part 1

Chromosome words.

Your DNA is a very long thread. It winds around proteins so it fits in the nucleus. You get one set of chromosomes from mom and one from dad.

  • Chromosome: one long DNA molecule wrapped around proteins.
  • Histones: the protein spools DNA wraps around. DNA plus histones = a nucleosome ("beads on a string").
  • Homologous chromosomes: a matched pair, one from mom and one from dad. Same genes, maybe different versions.
  • Sister chromatids: the two identical copies made in S phase, joined at the centromere.
  • Diploid (2n): two sets. Body cells. Humans: 2n = 46. Haploid (n): one set. Gametes. Humans: n = 23.

Memory trick

Sisters are twins: identical copies. Homologs are a couple: one from mom, one from dad.

Part 2

The cell cycle.

A cell spends most of its life in interphase getting ready. Then it divides in the mitotic (M) phase.

  • G1: the cell grows and does its normal job.
  • S: the cell copies (synthesizes) all its DNA.
  • G2: more growth, making proteins needed to divide.
  • M phase: mitosis splits the nucleus, then cytokinesis splits the cytoplasm.
  • G0: a resting state where cells stop dividing, like most nerve cells.
Memory trick

G1 = Grow. S = Synthesize. G2 = Get ready. M = Make two.

Cytokinesis looks different in animals and plants. Animal cells pinch in at a cleavage furrow (a ring of actin). Plant cells build a cell plate from Golgi vesicles, which becomes the new cell wall.

Bacteria divide by binary fission: copy the one circular chromosome, pull the copies apart, and split. No mitosis. The protein FtsZ forms a ring where the cell splits.

Part 3

Copying DNA.

This happens in S phase. DNA is a ladder. The two strands run in opposite directions (antiparallel), and the bases pair up in the middle.

  • Base pairs: A with T, and G with C. G-C pairs have 3 hydrogen bonds, A-T pairs have 2.
  • Purines (two rings): A and G. Pyrimidines (one ring): C, T and U.
  • Chargaff's rule: %A = %T and %G = %C.
Quick math

DNA is 30% A. Then T is also 30%. That leaves 40% for G and C, so G = 20% and C = 20%.

Copying is semiconservative: each new DNA molecule keeps one old strand and gets one new strand.

A crew of enzymes does the work at the replication fork:

  • Helicase unzips the two strands.
  • Primase lays down a short RNA primer so building can start.
  • DNA polymerase adds new bases. It can only build 5' to 3'.
  • The leading strand is built in one smooth piece. The lagging strand is built in short Okazaki fragments.
  • Ligase glues the fragments together.
  • Telomeres are repeats at chromosome ends. Telomerase rebuilds them in some cells, like stem cells.
Memory trick

Order at the fork: Helicase, Primase, Polymerase, Ligase. "Happy People Pop Lollipops."

Part 4 · Her focus

Counting math.

Exam questions ask how many chromosomes, chromatids or DNA molecules a cell has at some phase. You only need one picture and two questions.

  • Chromosomes: count the centromeres. An X shape is still 1 chromosome.
  • Chromatids: only exist after S phase. A copied chromosome has 2.
  • DNA molecules: every chromatid is 1 DNA molecule. Before S phase, 1 per chromosome.
  • DNA amount (c): G1 body cell = 2c. After S = 4c. Gamete = 1c.
Memory trick

A copied chromosome is one person in jeans: one waist (centromere), two legs (chromatids). Count heads, not legs.

The method, every time

  1. Write down 2n and n. If 2n = 8, then n = 4.
  2. Ask: has S phase happened? If yes, every chromosome has 2 chromatids, so chromatids and DNA molecules = chromosomes x 2.
  3. Ask: have the sisters split (anaphase)? If yes, every chromatid becomes its own chromosome, so the chromosome count doubles until the cell splits.
  4. After cytokinesis, split everything between the new cells.

The whole cycle on one table

Numbers for a cell with 2n = 4. "Daughter cells" means each new cell. Try it has a slider that changes the species.

Worked examples

Example 1

2n = 8. How many chromosomes, chromatids and DNA molecules at metaphase of mitosis?

  1. S phase happened? Yes. Sisters split? Not yet.
  2. Chromosomes = 2n = 8.
  3. Chromatids = 8 x 2 = 16. DNA molecules = 16.
Example 2

Human cell (2n = 46) at anaphase of mitosis. How many chromosomes?

  1. Sisters just split, so every chromatid counts as a chromosome.
  2. 46 x 2 = 92 chromosomes in that one cell.
  3. After cytokinesis each daughter has 46 again.
Example 3: DNA amount

A G1 cell has 6 pg of DNA. How much in G2? In each daughter? In a gamete?

  1. G1 is 2c, so c = 6 / 2 = 3 pg.
  2. G2 is 4c = 12 pg. Each daughter is 2c = 6 pg.
  3. A gamete is 1c = 3 pg.
Example 4: work backward

A cell at metaphase of mitosis has 24 chromatids. What is 2n?

  1. At metaphase every chromosome has 2 chromatids.
  2. Chromosomes = 24 / 2 = 12, so 2n = 12 and n = 6.
Example 5: doubling

One cell divides by mitosis 5 times, every cell dividing each round. How many cells?

  1. Each round doubles the cells: total = 2rounds.
  2. 25 = 32 cells.
Don't mix up n and c

n counts sets of chromosomes. c counts amount of DNA. S phase doubles DNA (2c to 4c) but the cell is still 2n, because the number of centromeres did not change.

Part 5

Mitosis phases.

Mitosis makes two identical cells with the same chromosome number as the parent. The drawings use 2n = 4 so you can count.

3D model · drag to spin

A cell going through mitosis (2n = 4)

  • Chromosomes from mom
  • Chromosomes from dad
  • Cell and spindle poles
  • Spindle fibers
  • Prophase: chromosomes condense and become visible. The spindle starts forming from the centrosomes.
  • Prometaphase: the nuclear envelope breaks down. Spindle fibers grab each chromosome at its kinetochore.
  • Metaphase: chromosomes line up in the middle, at the metaphase plate.
  • Anaphase: sister chromatids separate and move to opposite poles.
  • Telophase: new nuclear envelopes form, chromosomes relax, the spindle breaks down.
Memory trick

"Please Pass My Awesome Taco": Prophase, Prometaphase, Metaphase, Anaphase, Telophase. Metaphase = Middle, Anaphase = Away, Telophase = Two.

Easy to confuse

Centromere: the pinched middle of a chromosome. Kinetochore: the protein patch on the centromere that spindle fibers grab. Centrosome: the spindle's anchor at each pole.

Part 6

Control and cancer.

Cells get signals from other cells, then check themselves before each big step.

  • Signaling: a ligand binds a receptor. Paracrine = nearby cells. Endocrine = far away, through the blood (hormones). Autocrine = the cell signals itself.

There are three checkpoints. If something is wrong, the cycle stops.

  • G1 checkpoint: is the cell big enough, with enough energy and undamaged DNA?
  • G2 checkpoint: was all the DNA copied correctly?
  • M checkpoint (in metaphase): is every sister chromatid attached to the spindle?
  • Cyclins + Cdks are the gas pedal. Cyclin levels rise and fall; a Cdk only works when its cyclin is attached.
  • Rb, p53 and p21 are the brakes. p53 stops the cycle if DNA is damaged and can trigger cell death (apoptosis).

Cancer is cell division out of control. A proto-oncogene is a normal gas pedal gene; a mutation can turn it into an oncogene that stays on. A tumor suppressor gene (like p53) is a brake; losing it means nothing stops the cell.

Memory trick

ONCOgene: stuck ON. Tumor SUPPRESSOR: suppresses tumors, so losing it is bad. Cancer = stuck gas pedal + cut brakes.

Meiosis.

How the body makes sex cells with half the chromosomes, all a little different.

Part 1

Why meiosis.

Sperm and egg each need half the chromosomes, so when they join the baby has the right number. Meiosis makes those half cells.

  • Starts with one diploid (2n) cell. Ends with four haploid (n) cells.
  • DNA is copied once (S phase), then the cell divides twice: meiosis I and meiosis II.
  • The four cells are genetically different from each other and from the parent.
  • Fertilization: n + n = 2n. The joined cell is a zygote.
Memory trick

Half plus half equals whole. Mitosis makes twins; meiosis makes siblings.

Part 2 · Extra steps

What splits when.

This is the most tested idea. Each division pulls something apart. Learn which thing.

Meiosis I

Homologs split (the couples). Sisters stay together. The cell goes from 2n to n.

Meiosis II and mitosis

Sister chromatids split (the twins). The chromosome number in each new cell stays the same.

How to answer "what separates?"

  1. Find the division: mitosis, meiosis I or meiosis II.
  2. Meiosis I: homologs line up in pairs and the pairs split. Look for "tetrads" or "pairs at the middle".
  3. Mitosis or meiosis II: chromosomes line up single file and the sisters split.
  4. Only meiosis I has synapsis (homologs pairing) and crossing over.
Mitosis
  • 1 division
  • 2 cells, identical
  • 2n stays 2n
  • Growth and repair
Meiosis
  • 2 divisions
  • 4 cells, all different
  • 2n becomes n
  • Makes gametes (or spores)
Worked example

A picture shows homolog pairs lined up side by side at the middle. Which phase?

  1. Pairs, not single file, so it is meiosis I.
  2. Lined up in the middle = metaphase.
  3. Answer: metaphase I. Next step: homologs separate in anaphase I.
Memory trick

Meiosis I splits the couples. Meiosis II splits the twins. Mitosis only splits twins.

Part 3

The phases.

Same phase names as mitosis, done twice. Drawings use 2n = 4: purple from mom, amber from dad.

  • Prophase I: homologs pair up (synapsis) into tetrads and swap pieces (crossing over) at chiasmata.
  • Metaphase I: pairs line up at the middle, each pair facing a random way.
  • Anaphase I: homologs separate. Sisters stay joined.
  • Telophase I and cytokinesis: two haploid cells. No DNA copying before meiosis II.
  • Meiosis II: like mitosis in each cell. Sisters separate in anaphase II, giving four haploid cells.
Part 4 · Her focus

Counting in meiosis.

Same method as mitosis: write 2n and n, then ask "has S happened?" and "have the sisters split?". One more rule: after meiosis I, each cell has n chromosomes.

Numbers for 2n = 4. From telophase I on, numbers are per cell.

Worked example: 2n = 8
  1. Prophase I: 8 chromosomes, 16 chromatids, 4 tetrads (one per pair, so tetrads = n).
  2. After meiosis I, each cell: 4 chromosomes, 8 chromatids.
  3. Anaphase II, each cell: sisters split, so 8 chromosomes for a moment.
  4. Each gamete: 4 chromosomes, 4 DNA molecules.
Worked example: DNA amount

A G1 cell has 10 pg. Then c = 5 pg.

  1. After S (prophase I): 4c = 20 pg.
  2. Each cell after meiosis I: 2c = 10 pg.
  3. Each gamete: 1c = 5 pg.
Part 5

Why kids look different.

Three things shuffle the genes, so each gamete is unique.

  • Crossing over (prophase I): homologs swap matching pieces, making recombinant chromosomes.
  • Independent assortment (metaphase I): each pair faces a random way, like a coin flip. With n pairs there are 2n kinds of gametes.
Worked example
  1. Pea plant, n = 7: 27 = 128 kinds of gametes.
  2. Human, n = 23: 223 = 8,388,608 kinds, before crossing over.
  • Random fertilization: any sperm can meet any egg. For humans, 223 x 223 = about 70 trillion combos.
Memory trick

Cross, Flip, Meet: crossing over, the coin flip of assortment, random fertilization.

Part 6

Life cycles.

Every sexual life cycle switches between haploid and diploid. What changes is which stage is the big one.

  • Diploid-dominant (animals): the body is 2n. Meiosis makes gametes.
  • Haploid-dominant (most fungi, some algae): the body is n. The zygote is the only 2n stage.
  • Alternation of generations (plants): a 2n sporophyte makes spores by meiosis; a spore grows into an n gametophyte, which makes gametes by mitosis.

Part 1

Mendel's words.

Mendel crossed true-breeding purple and white pea plants. All the kids were purple. When those kids self-pollinated, white came back in about 1 of 4 grandkids. White was hiding, not gone.

  • P = parents, F1 = their kids, F2 = the grandkids.
  • Gene: a stretch of DNA for a trait. Allele: one version of a gene (P or p).
  • Genotype: the letters (PP, Pp, pp). Phenotype: the look (purple or white).
  • Homozygous: two of the same (PP or pp). Heterozygous: two different (Pp).
  • Dominant shows whenever present. Recessive only shows with two copies.
  • Law of segregation: the two alleles of a gene separate into different gametes.
  • Law of independent assortment: different genes sort into gametes independently.
Memory trick

GENOtype = the GENEs (letters). PHENOtype = the PHysical look. HOMO = same, HETERO = different.

Part 2 · Extra steps

Punnett squares.

A Punnett square shows every way the parents' gametes can combine. Each box is equally likely.

How to fill one

  1. Write each parent's genotype.
  2. List each parent's gametes: one letter per gene. Aa gives A and a. AaBb gives AB, Ab, aB, ab.
  3. Put one parent's gametes on top and the other's down the side.
  4. Fill each box with the top letters plus the side letters.
  5. Count the boxes for each genotype, then for each phenotype.
Memory trick

Gametes from AaBb: use FOIL like in algebra. First (AB), Outer (Ab), Inner (aB), Last (ab).

Worked example: Aa x Aa
  1. Gametes: A, a from each parent. 2 x 2 = 4 boxes.
  2. Boxes: AA, Aa, Aa, aa.
  3. Genotypes 1 AA : 2 Aa : 1 aa. Phenotypes 3 dominant : 1 recessive.
Know these by heart
  1. Aa x aa gives 1 Aa : 1 aa.
  2. AA x aa gives all Aa.
  3. AaBb x AaBb gives 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 (16 boxes).

A test cross finds a hidden allele. Cross the dominant-looking parent with a homozygous recessive (aa). All dominant kids means it was AA. About half recessive kids means it was Aa.

Part 3 · Extra steps

Probability.

Big crosses are faster with two rules than with a giant square.

Product rule: AND

Both things happen? Multiply. Heads AND heads = 1/2 x 1/2 = 1/4.

Sum rule: OR

One way or another way? Add. A 1 OR a 2 on a die = 1/6 + 1/6 = 1/3.

The method

  1. Split the problem one gene at a time.
  2. For each gene, do a tiny Aa x Aa style square and find the fraction you need.
  3. Multiply the fractions together (AND).
  4. If there is more than one way to get the answer, find each way and add them (OR).
Example 1

AaBb x AaBb. Chance of aabb?

  1. Aa x Aa gives aa = 1/4.
  2. Bb x Bb gives bb = 1/4.
  3. 1/4 x 1/4 = 1/16.
Example 2

AaBbCc x AaBbCc. Chance of showing all three dominant traits (A_B_C_)?

  1. Each gene: dominant look = 3/4.
  2. 3/4 x 3/4 x 3/4 = 27/64.
Example 3: uses OR

Aa x Aa. Chance of a heterozygote (Aa)?

  1. Way 1: A from mom and a from dad = 1/2 x 1/2 = 1/4.
  2. Way 2: a from mom and A from dad = 1/4.
  3. 1/4 + 1/4 = 1/2.
Example 4

Two carriers (Aa x Aa) have 3 kids. Chance all 3 are aa?

  1. Each kid is a fresh coin flip: 1/4 each.
  2. 1/4 x 1/4 x 1/4 = 1/64.
Shortcut

Kinds of gametes = 2h, where h = number of heterozygous genes. AaBbCC has 2, so 22 = 4 kinds.

Part 4

Beyond Mendel.

Many traits don't follow simple dominant and recessive. These patterns show up on exams.

  • Incomplete dominance: the heterozygote is in between. Red x white snapdragons give pink. F2: 1 red : 2 pink : 1 white.
  • Codominance: both alleles show fully, like AB blood.
  • Multiple alleles: more than two in the population. ABO blood has IA, IB and i.
  • X-linked: the gene is on the X. Males have only one X (hemizygous), so one recessive allele shows. Example: hemophilia, color blindness.
  • Lethal alleles: two copies kill, so carriers x carriers give 2:1 among survivors instead of 3:1.
  • Epistasis: one gene hides another. Albino mice (cc) make no pigment no matter what the color gene says.
Memory trick

INcomplete = IN between (pink). CO-dominance = both COme out. EPIstasis = one gene is the EPIc boss.

X-linked: carrier mom x healthy dad

Read the square
  1. Daughters: half are carriers, none affected.
  2. Sons: half are affected. Sons get their X from mom.
Part 5

Genes on chromosomes.

Genes sit on chromosomes, so they move the way chromosomes move in meiosis. This is the chromosomal theory of inheritance (Sutton and Boveri). Morgan backed it up with fruit flies.

  • Linked genes: close together on one chromosome, so they are usually inherited together.
  • Recombinants: new combos made by crossing over.
  • Recombination frequency = recombinants / total x 100. 1% = 1 map unit. The farther apart, the more crossing over. It never goes above 50%.
Worked example

450 parental and 50 recombinant offspring.

  1. 50 / 500 x 100 = 10%.
  2. The genes are 10 map units apart.

Nondisjunction is when chromosomes don't separate in meiosis. A gamete gets an extra copy or none.

  • Aneuploidy: one chromosome too many (trisomy, 2n + 1) or too few (monosomy, 2n − 1). Example: trisomy 21 (Down syndrome), XO (Turner), XXY (Klinefelter).
  • Polyploidy: whole extra sets (3n, 4n). Common in plants.
  • X inactivation: in females, one X per cell shuts down into a Barr body.
  • Structure changes: deletion (piece lost), duplication (piece copied), inversion (piece flipped), translocation (piece moved to another chromosome).
Memory trick

Picture ABCDEFG. Deletion: ABDEFG. Duplication: ABCCDEFG. Inversion: ADCBEFG. Translocation: ABC moves to another chromosome.

Part 1

The big picture.

A gene is a recipe kept safe in the nucleus. The cell makes a copy of the recipe (mRNA) and takes it to the kitchen (the ribosome) to make a protein.

  • T
  • G
  • C
  • Transcription: DNA to mRNA, in the nucleus.
  • Translation: mRNA to protein, at a ribosome in the cytoplasm.
  • This flow, DNA to RNA to protein, is the central dogma.
Memory trick

TRANSCRIPTion = writing a transcript, same language. TRANSLATion = translating into a new language, from bases to amino acids.

Part 2

The genetic code.

mRNA is read three letters at a time. Each three-letter word is a codon, and each codon means one amino acid or "stop".

  • 4 bases in groups of 3 gives 64 codons: 61 code for amino acids, 3 are stops (UAA, UAG, UGA).
  • AUG is the start codon. It codes for methionine (Met).
  • The code is degenerate (redundant): most amino acids have more than one codon.
  • The code is nearly universal: almost every living thing uses the same one.
Memory trick for stops

UAA: "U Are Away." UAG: "U Are Gone." UGA: "U Go Away."

Codon table

First letter on the left, second letter on top, third letter inside each box.

Part 3 · Extra steps

Transcription.

RNA polymerase reads one DNA strand and builds a matching mRNA. RNA uses U (uracil) instead of T.

  • Template strand: the strand RNA polymerase reads, 3' to 5'. The mRNA is its complement.
  • Coding strand: the other strand. The mRNA looks just like it, with U in place of T.
  • mRNA is always built 5' to 3'. No primer needed.

The three steps

  1. Initiation: transcription factors bind the promoter (often at the TATA box) and bring in RNA polymerase.
  2. Elongation: polymerase unwinds the DNA and adds RNA bases that pair with the template.
  3. Termination: polymerase reaches the end signal and lets go of the new RNA.
Worked example

Template DNA: 3'-TAC GGC ATT-5'. What is the mRNA?

  1. Pair each base: T to A, A to U, C to G, G to C.
  2. mRNA: 5'-AUG CCG UAA-3'.
  3. Check with the coding strand: 5'-ATG CCG TAA-3'. Same letters, T swapped for U.
Common mistake

An A on the template pairs with U in RNA, not T.

  • Eukaryotes have three RNA polymerases: I makes rRNA, II makes mRNA, III makes tRNA.
Memory trick

I, II, III = R, M, T: Ribosomal, Messenger, Transfer.

Part 4

RNA processing.

In eukaryotes the first copy is pre-mRNA. It gets three edits before it leaves the nucleus.

  • 5' cap: a modified G on the front end. Protects the mRNA and helps the ribosome find it.
  • Poly-A tail: about 200 A's on the back end. Protects the mRNA and helps it leave the nucleus.
  • Splicing: introns are cut out by spliceosomes, and exons are joined.
  • Alternative splicing: keeping different exons, so one gene can make several proteins.
Memory trick

EXons are EXpressed. INtrons stay IN and get tossed. Cap on the head, tail on the end.

Exam trap

Bacteria skip this. No nucleus, no introns, and they translate the mRNA while it is still being made.

Part 5 · Extra steps

Translation.

The ribosome reads the mRNA codon by codon. tRNAs bring the matching amino acids, and the ribosome links them into a protein.

3D model · drag to spin

A ribosome reading mRNA

  • Ribosome (small and large subunit)
  • tRNA
  • Amino acid
  • A
  • U
  • G
  • C
  • Ribosome: rRNA plus proteins, with a small and a large subunit.
  • tRNA: one end has an anticodon that pairs with the codon; the other end carries the amino acid.
  • Aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase: the enzyme that loads each tRNA with its amino acid.
  • A, P, E sites: A = Arrival, P = Peptide (holds the chain), E = Exit.

The three steps

  1. Initiation: the small subunit and the first tRNA (carrying Met) find the start codon AUG. The large subunit joins.
  2. Elongation: a new tRNA arrives in the A site, a peptide bond joins the amino acids, and the ribosome slides one codon. The empty tRNA leaves from E.
  3. Termination: a stop codon reaches the A site. A release factor binds, the protein is let go, and the ribosome comes apart.
Worked example

mRNA: 5'-AUG GCC UUU GGG UAA-3'. What protein, and what anticodons?

  1. Split into codons: AUG, GCC, UUU, GGG, UAA.
  2. Use the codon table: Met, Ala, Phe, Gly, stop.
  3. Anticodons are the complements: UAC, CGG, AAA, CCC. The stop codon has no tRNA.
Counting tip

Each amino acid needs 3 bases. A 300-base coding region (not counting the stop) makes 100 amino acids.

Memory trick

tRNAs move through the sites in order A, P, E. Think "APE": Arrive, Peptide, Exit.

Part 6

Mutations and repair.

A mutation is a change in the DNA sequence. Think of the sentence THE CAT ATE THE RAT.

  • Substitution (point mutation): one base swapped. It can be silent (same amino acid), missense (different amino acid) or nonsense (early stop codon).
  • Insertion or deletion: bases added or removed. If not a multiple of 3, it causes a frameshift that scrambles everything after it.
  • Mutagens like UV light and X-rays cause induced mutations.
  • Germline mutations (egg or sperm) can be inherited. Somatic mutations (body cells) can't.

Cells fix most mistakes:

  • Proofreading: DNA polymerase checks each base as it adds it.
  • Mismatch repair: enzymes fix wrong pairs right after copying.
  • Nucleotide excision repair: cuts out damaged DNA, like thymine dimers from UV light, and fills the gap.
Memory trick

Proofreading = fix while typing. Mismatch = fix after typing. Excision = cut out the burnt word and retype.

Gene control.

How cells turn genes up and down. Think of a dimmer switch, not an on/off switch.

Part 1

Why and where.

Making every protein all the time wastes energy. Turning genes up and down is also how one egg becomes hundreds of cell types. This is gene expression.

  • Prokaryotes (no nucleus): control is mostly at transcription.
  • Eukaryotes: control happens at five levels, from chromatin all the way to the finished protein.
Memory trick

"Every Tired Panda Takes Peace": Epigenetic, Transcription, Post-transcriptional, Translation, Post-translational.

Part 2 · Level 1

Chromatin (epigenetic).

DNA is wound on histone spools. Tight spools hide genes. Loose spools let genes be read. Epigenetic means a change in expression without changing the DNA sequence.

  • Histone acetylation: loosens the spools, so the gene is ON.
  • DNA methylation: methyl tags on cytosines (often CpG islands near promoters) pack DNA tight, so the gene is OFF.
Memory trick

Acetyl = Active (opens). Methyl = Muted (closes).

Part 3 · Level 2

Transcription.

Proteins called transcription factors decide how often a gene is copied into mRNA.

  • Promoter: the start line right before the gene. Often has a TATA box.
  • Enhancer: a DNA spot that can be far away. The DNA bends so an activator on the enhancer boosts transcription.
  • Repressor: a protein that blocks transcription.
Memory trick

Promoter = the starting line. Enhancer = a fan far away in the stands, yelling loud enough to make the runner go faster.

Part 4 · Level 3

After transcription.

Once mRNA is made, the cell can still change how much protein it gets.

  • Alternative splicing: different exons kept, so one gene makes different proteins.
  • mRNA stability: a longer-lasting mRNA makes more protein. RNA-binding proteins can make it last longer or break down faster.
  • microRNAs: tiny RNAs cut out by Dicer. They join RISC, find a matching mRNA, and destroy it or block it.
Memory trick

Dicer DICES the RNA. RISC is a RISKy place for an mRNA to be.

Part 5 · Levels 4 and 5

Translation and protein.

  • eIF-2 is a helper that starts translation. When the cell adds a phosphate to it (for example under stress), translation slows down.
  • Finished proteins get tags that change them: phosphorylation can switch them on or off.
  • A ubiquitin tag sends a protein to the proteasome to be destroyed. This is how cyclins get cleared.
Memory trick

Ubiquitin is a sticky note that says "Proteasome, please shred."

Link to cancer

Cancer cells often have genes turned up or down the wrong way, like too many growth signal receptors or tumor suppressors silenced by methylation.

Part 1

Gel electrophoresis.

To get DNA out of cells: break them open with detergent, remove proteins and RNA, then add cold alcohol so the DNA clumps. Then sort it on a gel.

  • DNA is negative (phosphate backbone), so it moves toward the positive end.
  • The agarose gel is like a sieve: small pieces travel farthest.
  • A ladder of known sizes lets you read each band's size.
  • Probes find a sequence on a blot: Southern = DNA, Northern = RNA, Western = protein.
Memory trick

"Run to red": DNA runs to the red (+) end. SNoW DRoP: Southern DNA, Northern RNA, Western Protein.

Part 2

PCR.

The polymerase chain reaction makes millions of copies of one piece of DNA. Each cycle has three temperature steps.

  1. Denature at about 95°C: the strands come apart.
  2. Anneal at about 50 to 65°C: short primers stick to each strand.
  3. Extend at about 72°C: heat-proof Taq polymerase builds the new strands.
PCR math

Copies double every cycle: copies = start x 2cycles. One copy after 10 cycles: 210 = 1,024. After 30 cycles: about 1 billion.

Memory trick

"Melt, Mate, Make": melt the strands, primers mate on, Taq makes the copy.

Part 3

Cloning.

Gene cloning puts a gene into bacteria so they make many copies. Cut, paste, put in, pick.

3D model · drag to spin

Cloning a gene into a plasmid

  • Origin
  • Antibiotic resistance gene
  • Restriction site
  • Your gene
  1. Cut: a restriction enzyme cuts the gene and a plasmid at the same sequence, leaving matching sticky ends.
  2. Paste: DNA ligase seals them together. This is recombinant DNA.
  3. Put in: bacteria take up the plasmid (transformation).
  4. Pick: grow them on an antibiotic. Only bacteria with the plasmid survive.
  • Reproductive cloning: Dolly the sheep, made by putting a body cell's nucleus into an egg with no nucleus (somatic cell nuclear transfer).
  • GMO: an organism with changed DNA. Transgenic means it has a gene from another species, like Bt crops that kill insect pests.
  • Gene therapy: adding a working gene to a patient's cells, often with a virus as the carrier.
Part 4

Genome maps.

  • Genetic (linkage) map: built from recombination frequency, like in Topic 3. Gives gene order and distance in map units.
  • Physical map: the real distance in base pairs.
  • They don't always match, because crossing over happens more in some places than others.
Memory trick

Genetic map = how often genes split up. Physical map = a ruler.

Part 5

Sequencing.

Sanger sequencing (chain termination) copies DNA with a few special "dead-end" letters mixed in. Each ddNTP has its own color and stops the copy because it has no 3' OH. Sort the pieces by size and read the colors in order.

  • Whole-genome shotgun: chop DNA into overlapping pieces, read them all, and let computers line them up.
  • Next-generation sequencing: machines read millions of pieces at once, fast and cheap.
Memory trick

DIdeoxy = DIe: the chain dies there.

Part 6

Uses.

  • Genomics: studying whole genomes. Helps predict disease risk (like BRCA genes).
  • Pharmacogenomics: picking drugs and doses based on someone's genes.
  • Metagenomics: sequencing DNA from a whole community at once, like soil or your gut.
  • Proteomics: studying all the proteins a cell makes.
  • Mitochondrial DNA comes only from mom, so it is used to trace ancestry.
Memory trick

PHARMacogenomics = your genes at the PHARMacy. METAgenomics = the whole crowd's DNA at once.
